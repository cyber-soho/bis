Part A

Comprehensive content review

A single fictional company runs through this whole module so you never have to learn a new scenario every five minutes: Northern Roast Coffee Co., a made-up Montréal coffee chain. Hour 1 of the module is plumbing — moving data between systems. Hour 2 is intelligence — turning that data into decisions.

Same coffee roaster, one new problem. Maya runs Northern Roast out of a converted garage: four cafés, two wholesale clients, eleven staff — and forty spreadsheets that no longer agree with each other. Inventory says forty kilos of Ethiopian beans; the café ran out yesterday. Payroll lives in one app, sales in another, supplier orders live in Maya's head. She does not need another spreadsheet. She needs an EIS (Enterprise Information System) — and the real question is not whether, but how to get one without bankrupting or breaking the company. That "how" is called acquisition, and it runs in five links. Notice the pattern in all five: each is mostly a people problem wearing a technology costume.

Module

Module 1 · Introduction to EIS and the Strategic Importance of IT

Most people, asked what an information system is, point at a computer. That is the trap. The computer is the part you can see; it is not the whole thing. An information system (IS) is the entire arrangement of people, machines, and rules that takes raw facts in at one end and pushes useful information out the other. You are an ingredient. So is the router blinking in the corner.

Every IS, from a corner café to a national bank, is assembled from the same six building blocks. Pull any one out and the whole thing stops being useful:

  • People — the humans who run it, use it, and read what it produces.
  • Hardware — the physical machines: screens, card readers, servers, routers.
  • Software — the programs that hold the rules and do the calculating.
  • Data — the raw facts: prices, names, quantities, dates.
  • Networks — the connections that let the parts talk to each other and the outside world.
  • Processes — the agreed steps and rules that tie everything together in the right order.

Here is the single idea to carry out of this chapter. A system climbs a ladder: it turns data (raw facts, like the number 4.50) into information (facts with meaning, "that latte costs four dollars fifty"), and over time into knowledge ("afternoon latte sales jump when it rains, so staff up"). Data → information → knowledge. That climb is the entire point of the machinery.

Anchor example — a $4.50 latte on Saint-Denis. You order, you tap your card, the machine beeps, a receipt prints, you leave. Three seconds. Now watch it in slow motion. The barista (people) taps the order on an iPad running Lightspeed (https://www.lightspeedhq.com/), a POS (Point of Sale — the till where a sale is rung up) platform built right here in Montréal; the POS app is the software. The iPad, card reader, and receipt printer are the hardware. The $4.50, the Québec sales tax on top, your loyalty points — that is data. Your card tap rides the café Wi-Fi (Wireless Fidelity — the wireless internet connection) and the wider internet, the networks, off to the bank and back with an approval in about two seconds. And the fixed order — ring up item, add tax, take payment, print receipt, lower the stock count by one — is the process. Six parts, one smooth purchase. Knock out the network and the payment never reaches the bank; knock out the process and you get charged tax twice. The parts feel invisible right up until one disappears.

Not everyone in a company needs the same information. The cashier needs the price of one item right now. The store manager needs to know how the whole store did this week. The chief executive needs to decide whether to open ten more stores next year. Same company, wildly different questions — so businesses stack different kinds of system in a pyramid.

Layer Stands for The question it answers Who uses it Northern-style example
TPS Transaction Processing System "Did this single transaction happen and record correctly?" Front-line staff, cashiers Every latte rung into Lightspeed (https://www.lightspeedhq.com/)
MIS Management Information System "How did we do this week or this month?" Store and department managers A weekly sales-by-category report
DSS Decision Support System "What happens if we change something?" Analysts, mid-level managers Modelling a price cut on pastries
ESS Executive Support System "Where should the whole company go next?" Executives, directors A dashboard of all stores plus market trends

Anchor example — one banana scan, climbing all four floors at Metro (https://www.metro.ca/). You scan a bunch of bananas, $0.79. That scan is a transaction, and the store's TPS records it instantly — price, time, store number. That night the MIS sweeps up thousands of those scans into one tidy report: produce sold $X today, bananas the top mover. Come planning season, an analyst opens a DSS and asks, "what if we drop banana prices ten percent during the winter slump?" The DSS models it. And up in head office, an executive glances at an ESS dashboard showing every store against the national grocery market, and decides whether bananas deserve more shelf space chain-wide. One banana, four floors. The detail shrinks as you climb; the size of the decision grows.

Imagine a company where every department bought its own software and never connected any of it. Sales keeps customers in one app, Finance in another, Shipping in a third — each with its own slightly different copy of "the customer." That is a silo: a tall, sealed container that does not share what is inside. Silos feel fine on day one and become a nightmare by month six, because the copies quietly drift apart. Sales has your new phone number; Finance has the old one; Shipping has last year's address. Whose version is right? Nobody knows.

An Enterprise Information System (EIS) fixes this by integration: instead of separate copies, every department reads and writes the same shared data. One customer record. One source of truth. Update it once, everyone sees it instantly. The big enterprise systems are usually named for what they integrate:

System Stands for What it integrates A name you might meet
ERP Enterprise Resource Planning Finance, inventory, operations, HR (Human Resources) SAP (https://www.sap.com/), Oracle (https://www.oracle.com/), Microsoft Dynamics 365 (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics-365)
CRM Customer Relationship Management Everything about customers and sales Salesforce (https://www.salesforce.com/)
SCM Supply Chain Management Suppliers, inventory, shipping, logistics Built into most major ERP suites
KMS Knowledge Management System Documents, expertise, lessons learned Company wikis and knowledge bases

Anchor example — Jean Roy orders a couch. In the silo version, four departments spring into action with four disconnected apps. Sales has "J. Roy" with a downtown postal code; Finance has "Jean Roy" at a different, older address; Shipping has a phone number disconnected last year. The couch goes to the wrong door, the delivery call fails, and everyone blames everyone — yet nobody made an obvious mistake. The system let four truths exist at once. In the integrated version, all four read and write one shared customer record inside an ERP; Sales fixes the phone, and Finance and Shipping instantly see it because there is only one record to see. The couch arrives first time. That is not luck; that is integration designed in.

Quick gut check: is the IT department a cost — a necessary expense, like the electricity bill — or a weapon the company fights with? For decades plenty of executives quietly treated it as the former. Then in 2003, Nicholas Carr lit a fire under that crowd with an article in the Harvard Business Review (https://hbr.org/) bluntly titled "IT Doesn't Matter." His argument, roughly: once everyone can buy the same computers and the same software, technology stops being a special advantage and becomes plumbing.

Here is the resolution most people land on, and it is the heart of this chapter: buying technology gives you nothing special on its own — anyone can buy it. The advantage comes from how cleverly you use it to do something competitors cannot easily copy. That is not plumbing; that is strategy.

To talk about strategy properly we borrow two famous tools from Harvard's Michael Porter (https://www.isc.hbs.edu/). The first, Porter's Five Forces, maps the pressures squeezing a business. Understand the five and you understand where your pain comes from — and where technology can push back:

Force What it means The IT lever Concrete example
Rivalry among competitors How hard direct rivals push for the same customers Use data to cut costs and personalise faster A recommendation engine that lifts the average basket size
Threat of new entrants How easily a newcomer can show up and steal share Build IT that's expensive or hard to copy (barriers to entry) A logistics network a startup can't replicate cheaply
Bargaining power of suppliers How much leverage your suppliers hold Connect many suppliers through one portal A supplier portal that lets you switch vendors quickly
Bargaining power of buyers How much leverage customers hold over price Raise switching costs with loyalty data A points program that makes leaving feel personal
Threat of substitutes How easily customers meet the need a different way Add digital features substitutes don't have A bank app with instant transfers vs. writing a cheque

Anchor example — Netflix (https://www.netflix.com/) and Amazon (https://www.amazon.com/). Netflix faces ferocious rivalry (every studio streams now) and fights it with a recommendation engine fed by your viewing data; it answers high buyer power (cancel in two clicks) by funding original shows you can't get elsewhere, which raises your switching cost. Amazon makes the threat of new entrants in retail look low because decades of warehouses and delivery software form a barrier to entry no newcomer can copy cheaply; its marketplace neutralises supplier power because no single seller is irreplaceable. None of that is plumbing — it is the five forces, answered in software.

The Five Forces look outward. Porter's second tool, the Value Chain, looks inward: how a company creates value, step by step. The idea is beautifully simple — a business is a chain of activities; each adds some value and some cost; what's left over when the customer pays more than everything cost to produce is the margin (the profit). In one line: margin = total value − total cost.

Porter splits the activities in two. Primary activities directly make and deliver the product: inbound logistics (getting materials in), operations (making the thing), outbound logistics (getting it out), marketing and sales, and service. Support activities prop those up: firm infrastructure, human resources, technology, and procurement.

The strategic punchline: IT can raise value or shrink cost at every single link in the chain. Picture a Montréal coffee roaster. Forecasting software in inbound logistics means fewer beans spoiling (cost down). Sensors on the roaster keep every batch perfect (value up, waste down). An online store widens marketing's reach (value up). A self-serve help page handles common questions (service cost down). Improve any link and the margin grows. IT is not off in its own corner; it runs through the whole chain.

Strategy talk is cheap until someone asks, "so what is it worth?" Two plain measures get you started.

  • Payback period answers the nervous owner's favourite question: "how long until I get my money back?" You add up the net cash the project returns each year until the running total climbs back above what you spent. The year it crosses zero is your payback.
  • ROI (Return on Investment) answers "for every dollar we spent, how many did we get back?" In its simplest form, ROI = net gain ÷ cost, expressed as a percentage. A five-year project that cost $100,000 and returned $150,000 net has an ROI of 50 percent.

These two are the on-ramp. Module 3 adds the more honest, time-aware measures — NPV (Net Present Value) and IRR (Internal Rate of Return) — that professionals reach for when the money is spread across several years. Bodies such as ISACA (https://www.isaca.org/) publish frameworks for governing and valuing IT investment so that "is it worth it?" gets a defensible answer rather than a hopeful one.

Module 1 in one breath. An information system is people, hardware, software, data, networks, and processes turning data into information and then knowledge. Businesses stack TPS, MIS, DSS, and ESS in a pyramid. "Enterprise" means integration — one shared source of truth. And IT earns its keep not by being bought but by being used cleverly: to answer Porter's five forces, to lift value or cut cost at every link of the value chain, and to clear the payback-and-ROI test that convinces the person holding the cheque.

Module 2 · Internal & External Integration, Business Intelligence and EIS

When each department keeps its data in its own private system that nobody else can reach, that is a data silo — picture a sealed grain silo with everyone else locked out. Silos feel tidy inside one team and quietly poison the whole company, because the same fact ends up stored in three places that slowly drift apart.

Anchor example — three slightly different Mayas. Maya Tremblay joins Northern Roast's loyalty program. Over a year, three departments each touch her record:

Name Maya Tremblay Maya Tremblay M. Tremblay
Email maya.t@email.com maya.t@email.com maya.tremblay@oldjob.com
Phone 514-555-0143 (blank) 514-555-0143
Lifetime spend $214 $190 $214
Address Plateau, Montréal Mile-End, Montréal Plateau, Montréal

A stale email in Finance, a missing phone in E-commerce, a disagreement about where Maya even lives. Send her a "we miss you" coupon and it lands in the wrong inbox, addressed to the wrong neighbourhood. Integration is the work of getting separate systems to share one consistent version of the truth, automatically, without a human re-typing numbers from one screen into another.

Move inside the company circle. Internal integration means the systems your own organisation owns and runs all share data automatically. The crown jewel is the ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) system — one large platform whose whole reason to exist is to keep finance, inventory, purchasing, and HR working off one shared database. Big names on the job: SAP (https://www.sap.com/), Oracle (https://www.oracle.com/), Microsoft Dynamics 365 (https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/dynamics-365). On the customer side, a CRM (Customer Relationship Management) platform such as Salesforce (https://www.salesforce.com/) keeps every interaction with a customer in one place.

The magic of internal integration: when one number changes, every system that depends on it updates by itself. Sell a 1 kg bag of house-roast beans at the Downtown till, and a single "sale" event ripples outward instantly — Inventory ticks the bean count from 40 down to 39, Accounting books $18.00 of revenue, and the reorder logic notices the count creeping toward the reorder point of 12 and keeps watching. One action, three synchronised reactions, no human re-keying anything.

Internal integration keeps your own house in order. External integration lets your house talk to other houses — suppliers, the bank, the shipping company, the tax authority, and customers. The hard part: you do not control the other side's systems, so both parties must agree on a shared language and a shared format in advance.

Historically that shared language was EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) — a strict, standardised format for swapping business documents (purchase orders, invoices, shipping notices) computer-to-computer, no email or paper in between. The format rules come from standards bodies: the North-American ANSI X12 standard (see ASC X12 (https://x12.org/) and the American National Standards Institute, ANSI (https://www.ansi.org/)) and the international UN/EDIFACT standard (curated alongside the International Organization for Standardization, ISO (https://www.iso.org/), with product identifiers from GS1 (https://www.gs1.org/)). Today much external integration also rides on APIs (Application Programming Interfaces) — modern web-based "doors" a partner's software can knock on — using web standards stewarded by the World Wide Web Consortium, W3C (https://www.w3.org/) and OASIS Open (https://www.oasis-open.org/). When Northern Roast files its sales taxes with the Canada Revenue Agency, CRA (https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency.html), that too is external integration.

Remember the reorder flag from Chapter 1.2? In a fully integrated chain, no human emails the supplier. The system assembles a purchase order itself and ships it across the company border in EDI or via an API. Internal data (stock) becomes an external transaction (an order), automatically. That border crossing is external integration.

So we want systems to share data. How do we physically connect them? Three patterns emerged over the decades, and you will hear all three named in any real IT shop:

  • Point-to-point (P2P) — wire each system directly to each other system. Simple for two or three; a tangled nightmare as you grow, because the number of connections explodes.
  • Hub-and-spoke — put one central piece of middleware (software whose job is to sit in the middle and pass messages) at the centre; every system connects only to the hub. Far fewer connections, one place to manage.
  • ESB (Enterprise Service Bus) — a more advanced shared "bus" systems plug into, which can also transform, route, and monitor messages as they pass. A hub-and-spoke that grew superpowers. Its underlying philosophy is SOA (Service-Oriented Architecture), where each capability is offered as a reusable "service."

The killer argument for leaving point-to-point behind is pure arithmetic. With n systems, point-to-point needs up to n × (n − 1) ÷ 2 connections; hub-and-spoke needs just n. At 4 systems that is 6 cables versus 4 — meh. At 12 systems it is 66 cables versus 12. Connections that grow like a square become unmaintainable; connections that grow in a straight line stay sane. That gap is the entire business case for middleware, hubs, and the ESB.

Integration is powerful, which means it is dangerous if done carelessly — connect two systems and one system's bug can spread to all the others. The professional safeguard is a data contract: an explicit, agreed definition of exactly what a message must contain and what each field must look like, so neither side can send garbage the other cannot read.

Anchor example. Northern Roast's supplier sends order confirmations automatically. One day a confirmation arrives with the quantity field missing and the price sent as the word "twelve" instead of the number 12. Without a gatekeeper, that nonsense flows straight into accounting. With a data contract, you validate every incoming message against the contract before you trust it — quantity must be a whole number, unit price must be a decimal — and you reject what does not fit. In production, "REJECTED" does not just print; it quarantines the message, alerts a human, and stops the bad data cold. This single discipline prevents a shocking share of real-world integration disasters.

"Just go with your gut" is fine for choosing lunch and terrible for running a business. BI (Business Intelligence) is the technologies and practices a company uses to collect, store, analyse, and present business data so people make evidence-based decisions instead of guessing. The research firm Gartner (https://www.gartner.com/), whose famous "Magic Quadrant" ranks BI tools, has tracked the industry for decades.

The vocabulary you will hear: a KPI (Key Performance Indicator) is a single number chosen with a target because it measures something that matters (revenue, average spend, units per sale). The four flavours of analytics climb in ambition: descriptive tells you what happened; diagnostic tells you why; predictive estimates what will happen next; prescriptive suggests what to do about it. Newcomers live mostly in the first two — which is exactly where you should start.

Anchor example — which store gets the renovation? Four managers each believe their store is busiest; everyone has a story. BI does not care about stories. Open the books — northern_roast_daily_sales.csv — and add up the revenue. Over a 14-day window the chain took in about $276,639 across 37,482 transactions. Broken out by store, the ranking is not subtle:

Rank Store Revenue (CAD — Canadian Dollars) Transactions
1 Downtown $100,776 13,531
2 Mile-End $67,737 9,221
3 Plateau $61,695 8,206
4 Westmount $46,432 6,524

Downtown does more than double Westmount's revenue. The renovation dollars have an obvious home, and nobody had to win an argument. That is BI.

That clean sales file did not start clean. In a real company raw data lives scattered across the operational systems — tills, web store, loyalty app, inventory — each in its own format, full of typos, duplicates, and quirks. BI needs that data gathered, scrubbed, and parked somewhere built for analysis. Three terms cover this plumbing:

  • A data warehouse (DW) is a large database built specifically for analysis and history — a single, clean, trusted source of truth, separate from the busy operational systems. The canonical design ideas come from the Kimball Group (https://www.kimballgroup.com/) and from Bill Inmon's body of work.
  • ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) is the three-step process that fills the warehouse: Extract data out of the source systems, Transform it (clean, standardise, fix types, remove duplicates), then Load it into the warehouse. You will also hear ELT (Extract, Load, Transform), which simply reorders the last two steps.
  • A data mart is a smaller, focused slice of the warehouse aimed at one department — a "sales mart," a "finance mart" — so each team gets a tidy view without wading through everything.

Why it matters: imagine the raw export with one store typed "downtown" and another "DOWNTOWN", a revenue value saved as the text "6 547.17", and the same transaction exported twice. Feed that to your ranking code and Downtown splits into two stores and the math breaks. ETL trims and Title-Cases the names, converts the messy text price into a real number, and collapses the duplicate — before analysis ever sees it. Analysts joke that 80 percent of the job is cleaning data, and they are not really joking. Real tools — Power Query (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/power-query/), Talend (https://www.talend.com/), cloud services — do this at massive scale, but the logic is exactly that.

A subtlety that trips up almost every newcomer: the database that runs the business minute-to-minute is not the database you should analyse. They have opposite jobs.

  • OLTP (Online Transaction Processing) is the operational database — the one behind the cash register. Its job is many tiny, fast reads and writes: "charge this card," "add this loyalty point," "decrement this stock." It must be lightning-quick and correct right now, and it holds mostly current data.
  • OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) is the analytical database — the one behind the BI dashboard. Its job is a few enormous questions over lots of history: "show me revenue by store by month for three years." It is optimised for slicing and summarising, not for the speed of individual updates.

The classic OLAP idea is the data cube: sales arranged along several axes at once — by store, by product, by time — so you can rotate and slice the cube to view any combination. Asking that kind of question against the live OLTP till would slow down real customers' payments. That is precisely why the warehouse exists: to let analysts ask big aggregating questions without hurting operations. (Example: build a cube of weekday vs weekend revenue per store, and the "do weekends justify extra staff?" question answers itself — without ever disturbing the live till.)

Everything so far has been backstage. This is what the business actually sees: the dashboard — a single screen of KPIs, charts, and summaries that tells a decision-maker, at a glance, how things are going. A good dashboard answers the top questions before they are asked. The market leaders you will meet are Microsoft Power BI (https://powerbi.microsoft.com/), Tableau (https://www.tableau.com/), and Qlik (https://www.qlik.com/) — but every one of them is, at heart, doing what a hand-built dashboard does. Northern Roast's vital signs over the 14-day window:

KPI Plain meaning Value
Total Revenue All money taken in $276,639
Transactions Number of sales 37,482
ATV (Average Transaction Value) Average spend per sale $7.38
UPT (Units Per Transaction) Items per sale 1.47

A regional director does not read CSV files. They open one screen, see four big numbers and a chart, and in five seconds know Downtown is carrying the chain and Westmount is lagging.

"BI" does not live alone; it sits inside a well-known hierarchy. As you climb the pyramid, data gets more summarised and the audience gets more senior:

  • TPS (Transaction Processing Systems) sit at the base — the OLTP systems that record raw operations (every sale, every payment). High volume, fine detail, frontline staff.
  • MIS (Management Information Systems) turn that raw activity into routine reports for middle managers ("last week's sales by store"). Summarised, scheduled, predictable.
  • DSS (Decision Support Systems) go further: interactive, model-driven tools that let analysts ask what-if questions and support a specific decision rather than just reporting the past.
  • EIS (Executive Information Systems) sit at the peak — highly summarised, visual, dashboard-style systems for senior executives who want the big picture in seconds and the ability to drill down into a number that surprises them. Today's executive BI dashboards are modern EIS.

The single sentence to remember: the higher you climb, the more summarised the data and the fewer, more senior the people looking at it.

Anchor example — the full arc. A cashier's single sale (TPS) flows through integration and ETL into a warehouse, gets summarised by OLAP, surfaces as KPIs on a dashboard (EIS), and finally feeds a DSS model: "If we cut Westmount's quiet weekday hours by 15% and pour the savings into a Downtown weekend promotion that lifts Downtown 8%, are we better off?" The model projects the chain ends up about $5,276 better off — a green light, tested before a single shift changed or a dollar was spent. That entire journey, from one tap on a screen to a boardroom decision, is Business Information Systems.

Module 2 in one breath. Lesson 1 (Integration) is how the data gets to one trustworthy place; Lesson 2 (BI & EIS) is how that data becomes a decision. Integration is the plumbing; BI is the tap you drink from. A company needs both, or it is either drowning in disconnected systems or starving for insight.

Module 3 · EIS Acquisition

The headline decision is almost always the same three-way fork. Build means you (or hired developers) write custom software from scratch — exactly what you want, owned forever, and "forever" is also how long you maintain it. Buy usually means COTS (Commercial Off-The-Shelf) software: a finished product (say an on-premises ERP suite) you license, install on your own servers, and configure to fit. Rent almost always means SaaS (Software as a Service) — software you access over the internet for a monthly fee, where the vendor runs the servers, backups, and updates. Maya does not own the building; she rents the apartment and the landlord fixes the plumbing.

Two more forks sit behind that one. CapEx vs OpEx: CapEx (Capital Expenditure) is a big up-front purchase you own as an asset; OpEx (Operational Expenditure) is an ongoing subscription you expense as you go. Buying leans CapEx; renting is pure OpEx — and for a cash-strapped young company the gap between a $120,000 cheque today and $28,000 a year can be the whole decision. And single-vendor vs best-of-breed: one big suite that does everything adequately, or the best specialist tool in each area plus the stitching you now own.

How do grown-ups choose between options that all look good in the demo? They refuse to decide on vibes. The professional tool is a weighted decision matrix: list the criteria that matter, weight each by importance so the weights sum to 100 percent, score each option 1–5, and let the arithmetic point at a winner. Its close cousin is TCO (Total Cost of Ownership), which counts the whole multi-year cost — licences, training, support, upgrades — not just the seductive sticker price. The cheapest system to buy is very often the most expensive to own. And when you go to market you meet three travelling acronyms: an RFI (Request for Information) is a casual "tell us what you've got"; an RFP (Request for Proposal) is a serious "here are our needs, propose a solution and a price"; an RFQ (Request for Quotation) is "we know exactly what we want, just quote it." Maya sends an RFP.

SaaS wins at 4.02. Building scores highest on fit and freedom-to-leave (of course — it is custom) but is slow, costly, and brutal on a small team. The matrix does not decide for Maya; it makes her trade-offs visible so she can defend the choice to her board.

A system does not buy itself, and — the part people forget — it does not succeed by itself either. More EIS projects die from confusion about responsibility than from any technical flaw. The classic killer is a sentence you have said yourself: "Wait — I thought you were doing that."

The cast: the Sponsor is the senior person who wants the project and controls the money (at Northern Roast, Maya wearing her CFO (Chief Financial Officer) hat). The Steering Committee sets direction and unblocks big obstacles. The PM (Project Manager) runs day-to-day schedule and budget. The BA (Business Analyst) translates "the café keeps running out of beans" into precise written requirements. The IT (Information Technology) Lead owns data, security, and integration. The Vendor sells and configures. The End Users — baristas and bookkeepers — will live in the software daily, and their opinion is asked last and matters most. The Change Manager worries about the humans: training, communication, and the real grief of losing a familiar tool.

To keep eight kinds of people from tripping over each other, use a RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) matrix: tasks down the side, people across the top, one letter per cell. R (Responsible) does the work. A (Accountable) owns the outcome and signs off — exactly one per task, because shared accountability is a polite name for no accountability. C (Consulted) gives two-way input before the work. I (Informed) is kept in the loop, one-way, after. The golden rule worth tattooing somewhere visible: exactly one A, at least one R, on every single row. Two A's and two people quietly contradict each other; zero R's and everyone assumes someone else is doing it. A good RACI is mostly empty space — fill every cell and you have invited too many people to too many decisions. The whole discipline echoes professional bodies such as the Project Management Institute, PMI (https://www.pmi.org/). Note the contract row carries an SLA (Service Level Agreement) — the vendor's written promise on uptime and support response times, negotiated by the PM and signed by the Sponsor.

This link quietly decides everything. The business case is the written, numbers-backed argument for why this project deserves the company's cash instead of, say, a fifth café. A sponsor does not approve an EIS because it is exciting; they approve it because the case shows it pays for itself. No case, no cheque.

A business case has two sides on a scale. Costs are easy to underestimate because the obvious ones hide the rest: up-front cost (licences, implementation, data migration, training) plus the long tail of annual costs (subscription/maintenance, support, upgrades) — which is exactly why you reach for TCO rather than the sticker. Benefits come in two kinds. Tangible benefits you can put a dollar on (staff hours saved, fewer stock-out errors, faster month-end reporting). Intangible benefits are real but hard to price (better decisions, happier staff, a more professional brand). A good case quantifies the tangibles honestly and names the intangibles without pretending to price them.

To compare money across years, know four ideas cold. ROI (Return on Investment) is net gain ÷ cost, as a percentage. NPV (Net Present Value) is more honest because it accounts for the time value of money — a dollar next year is worth less than a dollar today, so you discount future cash flows back to today's value and add them up; a positive NPV means the project earns more than your cost of capital. IRR (Internal Rate of Return) is the discount rate at which NPV would hit exactly zero — higher is better. And payback period is the plain-spoken favourite: how long until you get your money back (discounted payback is the most honest version).

Anchor example — Maya's five-year model. The system costs $120,000 to implement in Year 0 and $28,000 a year to run. Benefits: ~2,600 hours/year of manual reconciliation saved at a loaded rate of $32/hour ($83,200), plus $41,000/year from fewer stock errors and $18,000 from faster reporting — $142,200 annual benefit. She discounts everything at 10 percent.

If the business case is the optimist in the room, risk management is the friend who quietly asks, "okay, but what if it goes wrong?" This link is about identifying what could sink the project, deciding how worried to be, and planning what to do before the fire. Optimism gets the project approved; risk management gets it delivered.

A risk is a future event that might happen and would hurt if it did. Size every risk with one formula: risk score = probability × impact, each scored 1–5. A meteor hitting the data centre is catastrophic (impact 5) but tiny in probability (1), so its score is low; a staff member fat-fingering a data import is medium impact and high probability, so it climbs. Multiplying the two stops you over-worrying about dramatic-but-unlikely disasters and under-worrying about boring-but-frequent ones. Collect them in a risk register — a living table of each risk, its score, its severity band (High/Medium/Low), an owner, and a planned action — and picture it on a heat map, a 5×5 grid (probability one axis, impact the other) shaded green in the safe corner and red in the danger corner. Executives who will not read a spreadsheet will look at a picture.

For each risk you choose one of four responses — the "four T's": Reduce/Treat (lower its probability or impact), Transfer (push it to someone else, classically by insurance or the vendor's contract), Accept/Tolerate (when it is too small or too costly to bother with), or Avoid/Terminate (change the plan so the risk cannot occur). There is no shame in Accept — a mature team accepts small risks on purpose, to spend its energy on the big ones. Frameworks from ISACA (https://www.isaca.org/), NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) (https://www.nist.gov/), and ISO (https://www.iso.org/) (ISO 31000 on risk management) formalise this language.

The last link separates a competent professional from a merely clever one. Risk management asks "what could go wrong for us?" Ethics asks the bigger question: "what could go wrong for everyone else — customers, staff, society — and is this the right thing to do even if it is legal and profitable?" An EIS holds people's personal data and increasingly makes automated decisions about them. That is power, and power without ethics is just a faster way to do harm.

Start with privacy, because an EIS is a magnet for personal data. In Canada you must comply with PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act), enforced by the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada (https://www.priv.gc.ca/). With European customers you also meet GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation), overseen by the European Commission — Data Protection (https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection_en). Two principles do most of the work: data minimisation (collect only what you actually need — does a coffee loyalty app really need a date of birth?) and purpose limitation (use data only for the reason you collected it). Informed consent rounds it out — plain language, not a forty-page click-through nobody reads.

Then fairness and bias. Automated decisions (who gets credit, which shifts a worker is offered) inherit the bias in their data, at scale and with a false air of objectivity. The demands here are explainability (can the system say why it decided?) and bias testing (have you actually checked the outputs for unfair patterns?). Close behind is transparency — a documented, defensible procurement free of kickbacks and quiet conflicts of interest. Two more deserve a seat: accessibility means the system works for everyone, including staff with disabilities — the benchmark is the WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) from the W3C Web Accessibility Initiative (https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/) — and sustainability, the energy and carbon footprint of the data centres and the labour conditions in the technology supply chain. Treat ethics as a gate the project must pass before signing, not a sticker slapped on afterward.

Anchor example — Maya's ethics gate. She runs a ten-question self-assessment (each answered Yes = 2, Partial = 1, No = 0, weighted by seriousness) and scores 31 out of 42, or 73.8 percent — the middle band, "Proceed with conditions." Her weak spots are obvious from the "Partial" scores: consent wording, bias testing, accessibility, and data residency. So she neither cancels nor blindly signs; she fixes those four gaps first, then signs with a clean conscience. That is ethics working as a gate, exactly as it should.

Module 3 in one breath. Acquisition is not a purchase; it is a discipline of five linked decisions. Make the main decision with evidence (a weighted matrix → SaaS at 4.02). Give every task an owner with a RACI matrix. Prove the money with a business case (positive NPV ~$312,908, Year 2 payback). Face the danger with a risk register (3 High risks named before they bite). And refuse to sign until the project clears an ethics gate. In every link, the technology was rarely the hard part — the decisions, the people, and the judgement were.

Part C

Ten case studies with model answers

Work these the way you would work them in the exam room: read the scenario, cover the answer with your hand, and write your own response first. Only then compare. The "most defensible answer" below is not the only correct wording — it is the shape your answer should have.

Scenario. At 8:05 on a Monday, the internet drops at a busy Saint-Denis café. Suddenly the barista cannot take card payments, cannot look up prices on the iPad, and cannot tell how many croissants are left. The owner, panicking, shouts that "the computer is broken." A regular in line — who happens to study Business Information Systems — quietly disagrees.

Why the regular disagrees, and what actually failed. The "computer" is fine; the iPad still powers on. What failed is one of the six building blocks of the information system: the networks block. Because the café's POS (Point of Sale — the till where a sale is rung up) is a network-dependent system, losing connectivity severed the path that carried payment requests to the bank and pulled price and stock data from the cloud. Notice that several blocks are still healthy — hardware (the iPad), software (the POS app), and people (the barista) — yet the system as a whole stopped being useful, which proves the lecture's core claim: remove any one of the six blocks (people, hardware, software, data, networks, processes) and the system breaks. The professional fix is a process change: keep a charged phone hotspot and a paper backup within arm's reach so a single failed block does not halt the morning rush. The deeper lesson is that the six blocks feel invisible right up until one of them disappears.

Scenario. A grocery executive, a regional analyst, a store manager, and a cashier each describe a decision they made last week about bananas. The cashier "rang up a bunch at $0.79." The store manager "ordered three extra cases after seeing they were the top seller." The analyst "modelled whether a 10% winter price cut would pay for itself." The executive "decided to give produce more shelf space across all 200 stores after seeing the national trend."

Match each person to a layer of the pyramid and justify it. The cashier operates the TPS (Transaction Processing System) — recording one raw transaction the instant it happens. The store manager reads an MIS (Management Information System) — a summarised "top seller this week" report that rolls thousands of scans into a few useful lines. The analyst uses a DSS (Decision Support System) — a model-driven "what if we cut the price?" tool that supports one specific decision rather than reporting the past. The executive consults an ESS (Executive Support System) — a highly summarised dashboard blending internal performance with outside market data to steer the whole organisation. The pattern the exam wants you to state: as you climb the pyramid the data gets more summarised and the decisions get bigger, while the audience gets fewer and more senior. One banana, four floors.

Scenario. Jean Roy orders a couch from a Montréal furniture shop. Sales records "J. Roy" with a downtown postal code. Finance, from an older order, has "Jean Roy" at a different address. Shipping has a phone number disconnected last year. The couch is dispatched to the wrong address, the delivery call fails, and three departments blame each other in a heated meeting. The owner demands to know "who messed up."

Who messed up, and how should the shop prevent a repeat? The honest answer is that no individual messed up — and that is precisely the point. The failure lived in the system, not in a person: three disconnected applications (a set of data silos) allowed three contradictory copies of the same customer to exist at once, and they quietly drifted apart over time. The cure is an Enterprise Information System (EIS) built on integration — specifically an ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) approach where Sales, Finance, and Shipping read and write one shared customer record (a single source of truth). When anyone corrects the phone number, everyone sees the correction instantly because there is only one record to see. The exam-worthy sentence: silos are not a software bug; they are the default you get when each team keeps its own copy, and an EIS removes the conflict by design. That is why companies spend millions on enterprise systems — not for the software itself, but for the one truth it guarantees.

Scenario. A board member, fresh off reading Nicholas Carr's 2003 Harvard Business Review (https://hbr.org/) article "IT Doesn't Matter," argues the company should slash its technology budget because "everyone buys the same software anyway, so it gives us no edge." A strategist counters by pointing at Netflix (https://www.netflix.com/) and Amazon (https://www.amazon.com/).

How the strategist rebuts the board member using the Five Forces. Carr is partly right and the strategist concedes it: buying technology gives no edge, because anyone can buy it. But the advantage comes from how cleverly you use it to do something rivals cannot easily copy — and Porter's Five Forces show exactly where. Netflix faces brutal rivalry and answers it with a recommendation engine fed by viewing data; it faces high buyer power (cancel in two clicks) and raises switching costs with original shows you can only get there. Amazon turns the threat of new entrants into a near-impossibility with a logistics network so expensive it forms a barrier to entry, and neutralises supplier power through a marketplace where no single seller is irreplaceable. Each is a force answered in software. The strategist's closing line: cutting the IT budget blindly does not save money — it disarms the company in front of all five forces at once.

Scenario. A Montréal coffee roaster sells bags of beans for $18 that cost $9 to produce. The owner wants to lift profit but refuses to raise prices (customers would balk) and refuses a risky bank loan. A consultant says: "You don't need higher prices or more capital — you need technology placed at the right links of your value chain." The owner is skeptical and asks for one concrete number.

Where IT raises the margin, and the number that justifies it. Profit here is the margin = total value − total cost, and Porter's Value Chain says IT can raise value or shrink cost at every link. Concretely: forecasting software in inbound logistics means fewer beans spoiling in storage (cost down); sensors on the roaster keep every batch perfect in operations (value up, waste down); an online store widens marketing and sales reach (value up); a self-serve help page trims service cost. Each tweak nudges the margin up without touching the price. To justify the spend, the owner runs the two starter measures: payback period ("how long until I get my money back?" — count the years until cumulative net cash turns positive) and ROI (Return on Investment) = net gain ÷ cost, as a percentage. If a $20,000 forecasting-and-sensor package returns $12,000 of saved waste and added sales each year, it pays back in under two years and shows a healthy ROI — a defensible case the owner can act on without raising prices or borrowing. Bodies such as ISACA (https://www.isaca.org/) publish frameworks for valuing exactly this kind of investment.

Scenario. Northern Roast's marketing team sends Maya Tremblay a "we miss you" coupon. It goes to maya.tremblay@oldjob.com (her stale work address held only in Finance), is addressed to Mile-End (the neighbourhood the E-commerce system has) when she lives in the Plateau (per Marketing and Finance), and the SMS backup never sends because E-commerce has no phone number on file. The campaign manager assumes the email tool is buggy.

Diagnose the real cause and prescribe the fix. The email tool is not buggy; the company has a data silo problem. Three systems — the Loyalty App (Marketing), the Online Store (E-commerce), and Billing (Finance) — each hold a slightly different "Maya," and they were never forced to agree, so name, email, phone, and address drifted apart. The prescription is internal integration: bring those systems onto one shared ERP/CRM (Customer Relationship Management) record so a single, current "Maya" exists and any update propagates everywhere automatically. The exam-worthy insight: the bad outcome was not caused by anyone entering wrong data on purpose — it was the architecture (private copies) that guaranteed the drift. Integration is the work of making separate systems share one consistent truth, automatically, so a future coupon reaches the right inbox in the right neighbourhood the first time.

Scenario. Northern Roast started with four systems wired directly to each other — six connections, no big deal. Over a year they added e-commerce, loyalty, a data warehouse, and mobile ordering. Now the IT lead is woken at 2 a.m. roughly weekly because one of dozens of fragile direct connections has broken. A junior developer suggests "just keep adding the cables." The IT lead refuses and proposes a re-architecture.

Why the IT lead refuses, with the arithmetic, and what to build instead. Direct wiring is the point-to-point (P2P) pattern, and the reason to abandon it at scale is pure arithmetic: with n systems, point-to-point needs up to n × (n − 1) ÷ 2 connections, while hub-and-spoke needs just n. At 4 systems that is 6 versus 4 — fine. By the time Northern Roast hits 12 systems it is 66 connections versus 12 — and every one is a thing that can break at 2 a.m. The fix is to put middleware at the centre so each system connects only to a hub, or, better, to adopt an ESB (Enterprise Service Bus) — a shared "bus" that also transforms, routes, and monitors messages, built on the SOA (Service-Oriented Architecture) philosophy of reusable services. Connections that grow like a square become unmaintainable; connections that grow in a straight line stay sane. That single comparison is the entire business case for hubs and the ESB.

Scenario. Head office can fund a renovation at exactly one of Northern Roast's four stores this year. Each store manager insists theirs is busiest: the Plateau manager points to weekend lineups; Downtown points to foot traffic. The CEO is about to decide by seniority of the loudest manager. An analyst asks for two days and a copy of the sales history instead.

How the analyst settles it with evidence, and the pipeline behind the answer. This is textbook BI (Business Intelligence): replace the gut with evidence by asking a question, pulling the data, and letting the numbers answer. But the analyst cannot query the live tills — those are OLTP (Online Transaction Processing) systems whose job is fast, small, live sales, and a giant historical query would slow real customers' payments. So the data is first run through ETL (Extract, Transform, Load) — extracted from the messy operational systems, cleaned (Title-Casing "downtown" and "DOWNTOWN" into one store, turning the text "6 547.17" into a number, dropping a duplicated row) and loaded into a data warehouse built for analysis. There, an OLAP (Online Analytical Processing) query aggregates 14 days of sales into a ranking, surfaced on a dashboard of KPIs (Key Performance Indicators). The verdict: Downtown $100,776 vs Westmount $46,432 — Downtown more than doubles Westmount, so the renovation has an obvious home and nobody had to win an argument. The exam point: the clean, rankable file existed only because integration and ETL made it so; that unglamorous plumbing is what makes the executive's screen trustworthy.

Scenario. Maya, drowning in forty disagreeing spreadsheets, must choose how to acquire her first EIS. A developer friend offers to build a custom system; a vendor pitches an on-premises COTS (Commercial Off-The-Shelf) ERP for a $120,000 up-front cheque; a third vendor pitches SaaS (Software as a Service) at $28,000 a year. Maya's board wants a defensible decision, not a gut call, and wants to know whether the money makes sense at all.

How Maya decides, and how she proves the money. First she refuses to decide on vibes and builds a weighted decision matrix: eight criteria (fit and TCO weighted highest, demand-on-the-IT-team lowest), each option scored 1–5, weights summing to 1.00. The arithmetic returns SaaS at 4.02, ahead of Buy (3.10) and Build (3.01) — Build wins on fit and exit-freedom but is slow, costly, and brutal on her tiny team, while SaaS deploys fast and barely touches her IT capacity. The matrix does not decide for her; it makes her trade-offs visible and defensible to the board. Second, she proves the spend with a business case using TCO (Total Cost of Ownership) rather than the sticker price, and a five-year cash-flow model discounted at 10%: $120,000 in Year 0 plus $28,000/year against $142,200/year of benefit yields a TCO of $260,000, total benefits of $711,000, a positive NPV (Net Present Value) of about $312,908, an ROI (Return on Investment) of ~173%, and discounted payback partway through Year 2. A sponsor seeing a positive NPV with a two-year payback says yes. Note she also weighed CapEx vs OpEx — and for a cash-strapped young company, OpEx (the subscription) is far gentler than a six-figure cheque. The measures she used are taught by trainers such as the Corporate Finance Institute (https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/).

Scenario. The SaaS contract is on Maya's desk, pen in hand. Her business case is strong. But during the project two things happened: a task ("migrate and validate the customer data") had two people who each thought they owned it and quietly contradicted each other, and a quick ethics review flagged that the loyalty app collects customers' dates of birth it never uses, the consent wording is buried in a forty-page click-through, and the vendor's automated shift-scheduling feature has never been tested for bias. Maya puts the pen down.

Why Maya does not sign, and the three disciplines she applies first. First, roles and responsibilities: the "two owners" problem is exactly what a RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) matrix exists to kill. The golden rule is exactly one A and at least one R per task — two A's mean two people think they are in charge, which is how data migrations go wrong. Maya re-draws the RACI so the PM (Project Manager) is the single Accountable party for migration, with the IT Lead and Vendor Responsible. Second, risk management: she checks her risk register, where "data migration corrupts records" already scored 15 (High) and "low user adoption" scored 16 (High); she confirms both have owners and a Reduce response, and that the breach risk is Transferred via cyber insurance. Third — and decisively — ethics as a gate: collecting unused dates of birth violates data minimisation, the buried consent fails informed consent, and the untested scheduler fails bias testing and explainability, all under PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act), enforced by the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada (https://www.priv.gc.ca/). Her ethics self-assessment lands at 73.8% — "Proceed with conditions." So Maya neither cancels the project nor blindly signs: she fixes the consent wording, stops collecting the unused birthdays, runs the bias test, and then signs. The exam-worthy principle: ethics is a gate the project passes before signing, not a sticker applied afterward — and across roles, risk, and ethics alike, the technology was never the hard part; the people and the judgement were.
