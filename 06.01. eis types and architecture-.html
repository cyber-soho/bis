Lesson 1

EIS Types and Architecture

Three things, in plain language: the basic EIS (Enterprise Information System) types, a little background on where they came from, and the key components and properties of the two systems you'll meet most often — ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) and CRM (Customer Relationship Management).

Warm-up

Setting the scene: what problem are we even solving?

Warm-up · Setting the scene: what problem are we even solving?

Picture your favourite coffee chain on a Monday morning. Behind that one cappuccino sit a dozen invisible questions. Do we have enough beans? Did we pay the supplier? Is this customer a regular who's earned a free drink today? Which store sold the most last week? No single person keeps all of that in their head. The business runs on information — and the software that organises that information is what this whole course is about.

An EIS (Enterprise Information System) is simply a large piece of software that helps a whole organisation — not just one person — collect, store, and use its information to get work done and make decisions. "Enterprise" is just a grown-up word for "the entire organisation," as opposed to one team or one laptop.

Two words that get mixed up constantly, so let's settle them now:

  • IT (Information Technology) is the gear and the plumbing — the computers, networks, and cables.
  • IS (Information System) is the whole arrangement — the technology plus the people, the data, and the steps they follow to turn raw facts into something useful.

A cash register is IT. The register, the cashier, the price list, and the routine they follow to take your money and update the day's takings — that's an IS. Hold on to that difference; the rest of the lesson leans on it.

Now let's see how these systems grew up.

Part A

Background: how enterprise systems grew up

Part A · Background: how enterprise systems grew up

Enterprise software didn't arrive fully formed. It crept forward one practical headache at a time, and every step solved the problem the last step created. You'll understand today's systems far better if you know the story, because the story is really one idea repeated: each generation let more of the business share the same trustworthy information.

Here's the short version.

It started on paper. Back in 1913, an engineer named Ford W. Harris worked out a tidy formula called EOQ (Economic Order Quantity) — basically, "what's the cheapest amount of stock to order at once?" No computers, just math on a page. The point is that businesses were already trying to make smarter decisions with numbers.

Fast-forward to the 1960s and computers entered the back office. Factories started using MRP (Material Requirements Planning) to answer a tricky question: if I want to build 500 bicycles next month, exactly which parts do I need, and when? MRP turned a sales plan into a shopping list and a schedule.

By the 1980s that idea grew into MRP II (Manufacturing Resource Planning), which folded in more of the factory — not just parts, but machine time, labour, and costs. Different departments were starting to plug into the same system instead of keeping separate notebooks.

Then in the 1990s the big leap happened. A research firm called Gartner coined the term ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) to describe software that ties every major department — finance, sales, inventory, human resources — into one connected system sharing one database. Suddenly the whole company could read from the same page.

The 2000s pushed those connections outward to suppliers and customers (sometimes called ERP II), and from the 2010s onward everything moved to the cloud (software you rent over the internet instead of running on your own servers) with a growing dose of AI (Artificial Intelligence) for forecasting and automation.

Notice the pattern: paper math, then one factory function, then the whole factory, then the whole company, then the whole supply chain, then the cloud. More integration every single time. That's the heartbeat of this entire field.

Real-life example — "The three notebooks that never agreed"

What this example shows: a small business outgrowing scattered, separate records — exactly the pain that pushed companies from paper to MRP to ERP. How it ties to the lecture: it makes the abstract idea of "more integration over time" concrete, so the timeline above stops being a list of dates and becomes a story you can feel.

Imagine Maya's Bikes, a little shop near campus. In year one, Maya keeps three notebooks. One for sales, one for parts she's ordered, and one for money in and out. It works — barely.

Then business booms. A customer asks, "Do you have the blue tires in stock?" Maya checks the parts notebook: yes, 4 left. She sells them. But she forgets to cross them off, because she's busy. An hour later she promises the same 4 tires to someone else. Now she's oversold, one customer is angry, and her money notebook doesn't match her parts notebook.

The notebooks aren't the problem. The problem is that the same fact ("4 tires left") lives in three places and nobody keeps them in sync. Every business in history has hit this wall. MRP, MRP II, and finally ERP were invented to knock it down — by storing each fact once, in a shared place everyone reads from. That single idea is the spine of Part C later on.

Part B

The basic EIS types: the information pyramid

Part B · The basic EIS types: the information pyramid

Now the heart of "basic EIS types." Inside almost any organisation, information systems stack up like a pyramid. At the bottom there's a flood of tiny day-to-day events; as you climb, those events get squeezed into summaries, then into decisions. Four classic layers show up again and again. Let's meet them from the ground up.

Layer 1 — TPS (Transaction Processing System). This is the engine room. A transaction is just one small business event: a sale, a payment, a booking, a clock-in. A TPS records these fast and accurately, thousands of times a day. When you tap your card to buy a coffee, a TPS logs it. It doesn't analyse anything — it just captures the raw facts, perfectly, every time.

Layer 2 — MIS (Management Information System). Nobody wants to read 4,000 individual sales. An MIS takes the pile of transactions from the TPS and summarises them into reports a manager can skim — "today's total sales were \$3,200, lattes were the top seller." It answers what happened? It deals in routine, structured summaries.

Layer 3 — DSS (Decision Support System). A DSS goes one step further. It takes the summarised information, mixes in extra knowledge or some "what-if" modelling, and helps someone make a choice. If we drop the price of cappuccinos by 10%, what might happen to profit? It supports a decision rather than just reporting the past.

Layer 4 — ESS (Executive Support System). At the top sit the executives — the chief executive and other senior leaders. An ESS (also called an EIS in some textbooks, confusingly, for Executive Information System) gives them a high-level dashboard: a few big trends, comparisons against rivals, the long view. It answers where is this whole organisation heading? and it leans on outside information about markets and competitors, not just internal data.

The golden rule of the pyramid: data flows up, and gets refined at every step. Raw facts at the bottom become information in the middle and decisions near the top. Fewer people, bigger picture, longer time horizon as you rise.

A quick note to avoid a classic mix-up: in this course, "EIS" in our title means Enterprise Information System (the big umbrella term for all business systems). Some books also use "EIS" for Executive Information System, the top layer of this pyramid. Same three letters, different scope. When in doubt, ask "enterprise-wide, or just the executives?"

Real-life example — "One coffee sale climbs the pyramid"

What this example shows: a single coffee sale travelling up all four layers, changing form at each step from a raw fact into a business decision. How it ties to the lecture: it makes the pyramid move. Instead of four definitions, you see one piece of data being refined as it rises — the golden rule in action.

Follow one cup. TPS: at 8:02 a.m., a customer buys two lattes; the till records "2 × Latte, \$9.00, 08:02." Just the facts. MIS: at closing, the manager pulls today's report — "142 drinks sold, \$612 in revenue, lattes on top." All those individual sales boiled down to a few lines. DSS: the manager wonders whether to run a morning latte promo; the DSS models it — "a 10% morning discount could lift latte volume ~15% but trim margin; net effect: slightly positive." ESS: the regional director never sees a single latte. Her dashboard shows "Store #7 revenue up 4% this quarter, ahead of the city average."

Same coffee, four very different views — because each layer exists to serve a different person asking a different size of question.

Code illustration — watch the data climb (Python)

What this code does: follows one day of coffee sales up three layers of the pyramid in actual code — the TPS records every sale, the MIS turns them into a manager's report, and the DSS recommends whether to re-order beans. How it ties to the lecture: it's the pyramid you just read about, running. You can literally watch raw facts become a report and then a decision.

B_pyramid_dataflow.pyPython
#!/usr/bin/env python3
# =====================================================================
# TASK OF THIS EXAMPLE
# =====================================================================
# Follow ONE coffee sale as it climbs the information-systems pyramid:
#   * TPS records each raw sale,
#   * MIS turns the day's sales into a manager's report,
#   * DSS uses that report to recommend whether to re-order beans.
#
# HOW IT RELATES TO THE LECTURE
# This is a hands-on version of the "EIS pyramid" in Lesson 1. It shows
# the golden rule of the pyramid: data flows UP and is refined at each
# level — raw transactions become information, and information becomes a
# decision. Front-line staff use the TPS; a middle manager reads the MIS
# report; a senior manager trusts the DSS recommendation.
#
# Pure standard library — runs offline on any computer with Python 3.
#   Run it with:   python3 B_pyramid_dataflow.py
# =====================================================================

# We only need Python's built-in tools, so nothing has to be installed.
from datetime import date          # used to stamp the report with today's date
from collections import Counter    # a quick way to tally items by name

# -------- LEVEL 1: TPS (Transaction Processing System) --------
# A TPS just records the plain facts of each sale. Here, each sale is a
# small dictionary: which drink, how many, and the price each.
RAW_SALES = [
    {"item": "Latte",      "qty": 2, "price": 4.50},   # a single transaction
    {"item": "Espresso",   "qty": 1, "price": 3.00},   # another transaction
    {"item": "Latte",      "qty": 3, "price": 4.50},   # the TPS keeps every one
    {"item": "Cappuccino", "qty": 2, "price": 4.25},
    {"item": "Latte",      "qty": 1, "price": 4.50},
    {"item": "Espresso",   "qty": 2, "price": 3.00},
]

# Beans used per drink (grams). The DSS will need this to judge stock.
BEANS_PER_DRINK = {"Latte": 18, "Espresso": 9, "Cappuccino": 16}

# How many grams of beans the shop has in the cupboard right now.
BEANS_IN_STOCK = 250

# -------- LEVEL 2: MIS (Management Information System) --------
# The MIS does NOT show every sale. It SUMMARISES them into a report that
# a manager can read in seconds.
def build_mis_report(sales):
    # Start a running total of money taken.
    total_revenue = 0.0
    # Start a counter that will tally how many of each drink were sold.
    drinks_sold = Counter()
    # Look at every raw transaction the TPS recorded.
    for sale in sales:
        # Add this sale's money (quantity x price) to the running total.
        total_revenue += sale["qty"] * sale["price"]
        # Add this sale's quantity to the tally for that drink.
        drinks_sold[sale["item"]] += sale["qty"]
    # Hand back the summarised numbers as a tidy dictionary.
    return {"revenue": total_revenue, "drinks_sold": drinks_sold}

# -------- LEVEL 3: DSS (Decision Support System) --------
# The DSS takes the MIS summary, mixes in extra knowledge (beans per drink
# and current stock), and RECOMMENDS an action.
def dss_reorder_advice(report, beans_recipe, stock):
    # Work out how many grams of beans today's sales actually used.
    beans_used = sum(beans_recipe[d] * n for d, n in report["drinks_sold"].items())
    # Calculate what is left in the cupboard after today.
    beans_left = stock - beans_used
    # Business rule: if less than 100 g remains, advise re-ordering.
    should_reorder = beans_left < 100
    # Return the analysis AND a clear recommendation.
    return {"beans_used": beans_used, "beans_left": beans_left, "reorder": should_reorder}

# -------- Run the three levels in order and print the journey --------
# This block only runs when the file is executed directly.
if __name__ == "__main__":
    # LEVEL 1 output: confirm how many raw transactions the TPS holds.
    print("TPS  (raw transactions recorded):", len(RAW_SALES), "sales\n")

    # LEVEL 2: build and print the manager's report.
    mis = build_mis_report(RAW_SALES)
    print("MIS  (daily report for", date.today(), ")")
    print(f"   Total revenue: ${mis['revenue']:.2f}")
    for drink, count in mis["drinks_sold"].most_common():   # best-sellers first
        print(f"   {drink:<12} sold: {count}")
    print()

    # LEVEL 3: turn the report into a decision.
    dss = dss_reorder_advice(mis, BEANS_PER_DRINK, BEANS_IN_STOCK)
    print("DSS  (decision support)")
    print(f"   Beans used today: {dss['beans_used']} g")
    print(f"   Beans remaining:  {dss['beans_left']} g")
    # Translate the True/False flag into advice a human can act on.
    if dss["reorder"]:
        print("   RECOMMENDATION: Re-order beans now (stock below 100 g).")
    else:
        print("   RECOMMENDATION: Stock is fine — no action needed.")

# =====================================================================
# STEP-BY-STEP, IN PLAIN ENGLISH
# =====================================================================
# 1. RAW_SALES is our TPS: a faithful list of every single sale. That is
#    all a TPS does — it records facts quickly and accurately.
# 2. build_mis_report() is our MIS: it loops over those raw sales and
#    boils them down to two numbers a manager cares about — total money
#    taken and how many of each drink sold. It hides the detail.
# 3. dss_reorder_advice() is our DSS: it takes the MIS summary, adds
#    outside knowledge (the bean recipe and current stock), and applies a
#    rule to RECOMMEND whether to re-order. It supports a decision.
# 4. The main block runs the three steps in order, so you can literally
#    watch one day's data climb the pyramid: facts -> report -> decision.
# 5. Try changing BEANS_IN_STOCK to 90 and re-run: the same data now
#    produces the opposite recommendation. That is decision support in
#    action — the system reacts to the situation.
# =====================================================================

Part C

Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP): the shared-database backbone

Part C · Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP): the shared-database backbone

Remember Maya and her three notebooks that never agreed? ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) is the grown-up cure. An ERP system ties a company's core departments — finance, sales, inventory, purchasing, manufacturing, human resources — into one integrated system built around one shared database. Instead of each team keeping its own copy of the facts, everyone reads from and writes to the same central record.

That last sentence is the whole point, so let's be crystal clear about the key components and properties of ERP.

The defining property: one shared database. This is what makes ERP ERP. When the sales team sells five mugs, the inventory count drops by five instantly and automatically, the finance ledger gains the revenue, and the reporting tools see the change — all without anyone re-typing anything anywhere. One fact, stored once, true everywhere. No more notebooks fighting each other.

The components: modules. An ERP isn't one giant blob; it's a set of modules (think of them as apps that all plug into the same database). A company switches on the modules it needs:

  • Finance & Accounting — the money: the general ledger, AP (Accounts Payable) which is money the company owes, and AR (Accounts Receivable) which is money owed to the company.
  • Inventory & Manufacturing — what's in the warehouse and what's being built.
  • Sales & Order Management — quotes, orders, and invoices.
  • Procurement (Purchasing) — buying raw materials and supplies.
  • HRM (Human Resource Management) — employees, payroll, and time-off.
  • BI (Business Intelligence) — dashboards and reports that read across all the other modules.

Because every module shares the same data, a question like "did we make a profit on last week's bike orders?" can be answered in seconds, pulling from sales, inventory, and finance at once.

Other key properties worth knowing:

  • Best-practice workflows — ERP software ships with sensible, standardised ways of doing common tasks, nudging the whole company onto one consistent process.
  • Real-time visibility — because the data is shared and live, managers see what's happening now, not last month.
  • Scalability — you can start with a few modules and add more as the business grows.

Who makes ERP software? The big names you'll hear are SAP, Oracle (including its NetSuite product), and Microsoft Dynamics 365. For a vendor-neutral explainer, the technology company IBM keeps a readable overview. These systems run everything from corner shops to airlines; the global ERP market is worth roughly \$80 billion a year, which tells you how central this idea has become.

That reliability is what companies are really buying when they buy ERP.

Code illustration — one shared database, many modules (Python)

What this code does: models a tiny ERP. Three "modules" (Sales, Inventory, Finance) plus a BI view all share one database object. A single sale updates that one database, and every module instantly reflects it — with no data re-entered anywhere. How it ties to the lecture: it's the defining property of ERP — one shared database — turned into something you can run and verify.

Plain Python, no libraries needed. Save as C_erp_shared_database.py (also a download) and run python3 C_erp_shared_database.py.

C_erp_shared_database.pyPython
#!/usr/bin/env python3
# =====================================================================
# TASK OF THIS EXAMPLE
# =====================================================================
# Show the ONE idea that makes ERP powerful: a single shared database.
# When the Sales module records a sale, the Inventory, Finance and
# Business-Intelligence modules all see the change immediately, because
# they read from the SAME data — not from separate copies.
#
# HOW IT RELATES TO THE LECTURE
# This is the "key components and properties" of ERP from Lesson 1, made
# concrete. The property on display is the "single source of truth":
# every module shares one database, so data is entered once and is
# instantly consistent everywhere. We also show the OLD way (siloed
# copies) to make the contrast obvious.
#
# Pure standard library — runs offline on any computer with Python 3.
#   Run it with:   python3 C_erp_shared_database.py
# =====================================================================

# ---- The shared database: one object every module talks to ----
# Think of this class as the central ERP database in the middle of the
# hub-and-spoke diagram. All modules call its methods.
class SharedDatabase:
    # When the database is created, set up its starting contents.
    def __init__(self):
        # A product catalogue: product name -> {stock on hand, unit price}.
        self.products = {
            "Coffee Mug":  {"stock": 40, "price": 12.00},
            "T-Shirt":     {"stock": 25, "price": 20.00},
        }
        # An empty list that will hold every sale, shared by all modules.
        self.sales = []
        # A running cash figure the Finance module will update.
        self.cash = 0.0

    # The Sales module calls this ONE method to record a sale.
    def record_sale(self, product, qty):
        # Refuse the sale if we do not have enough stock (a basic rule).
        if self.products[product]["stock"] < qty:
            raise ValueError(f"Not enough {product} in stock")
        # Look up the price from the shared catalogue.
        price = self.products[product]["price"]
        # Reduce the stock — Inventory will now see the lower number.
        self.products[product]["stock"] -= qty
        # Calculate the money for this sale.
        amount = qty * price
        # Add the money to cash — Finance will now see the higher number.
        self.cash += amount
        # Save the sale itself so Business Intelligence can analyse it.
        self.sales.append({"product": product, "qty": qty, "amount": amount})
        # Tell the caller the sale succeeded and for how much.
        return amount

# ---- The modules: each is just a "view" onto the same database ----

# The Sales module: its job is to write new sales into the shared DB.
class SalesModule:
    # Keep a reference to the one shared database.
    def __init__(self, db): self.db = db
    # Make a sale by asking the shared database to record it.
    def sell(self, product, qty):
        amount = self.db.record_sale(product, qty)
        print(f"[Sales]     Sold {qty} x {product} for ${amount:.2f}")

# The Inventory module: it only READS stock from the same shared DB.
class InventoryModule:
    def __init__(self, db): self.db = db
    # Report current stock straight from the shared catalogue.
    def stock_report(self):
        print("[Inventory] Current stock:")
        # Walk through each product in the shared catalogue.
        for name, info in self.db.products.items():
            print(f"            {name:<12} {info['stock']} left")

# The Finance module: it reads the same cash figure the sale updated.
class FinanceModule:
    def __init__(self, db): self.db = db
    # Show total cash taken, read directly from the shared database.
    def cash_position(self):
        print(f"[Finance]   Cash on hand: ${self.db.cash:.2f}")

# The Business-Intelligence module: it analyses the shared sales list.
class BIModule:
    def __init__(self, db): self.db = db
    # Find the best-selling product from the same shared sales records.
    def best_seller(self):
        # If no sales yet, say so and stop.
        if not self.db.sales:
            print("[BI]        No sales yet to analyse"); return
        # Tally units sold per product across all shared sales.
        totals = {}
        for s in self.db.sales:
            totals[s["product"]] = totals.get(s["product"], 0) + s["qty"]
        # Pick the product with the highest tally.
        top = max(totals, key=totals.get)
        print(f"[BI]        Best seller so far: {top} ({totals[top]} units)")

# ---- Run the scene: one sale, four modules, all in agreement ----
if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Create the single shared database.
    db = SharedDatabase()
    # Plug every module into that SAME database.
    sales, inventory, finance, bi = SalesModule(db), InventoryModule(db), FinanceModule(db), BIModule(db)

    print("=== BEFORE the sale ===")
    inventory.stock_report()      # 40 mugs
    finance.cash_position()       # $0.00
    print()

    # The Sales module records a sale ONCE.
    print("=== Sales records one transaction ===")
    sales.sell("Coffee Mug", 5)
    print()

    # Without anyone re-typing anything, every other module is up to date.
    print("=== AFTER the sale (no data re-entered anywhere) ===")
    inventory.stock_report()      # now 35 mugs  <- Inventory already knows
    finance.cash_position()       # now $60.00    <- Finance already knows
    bi.best_seller()              # Coffee Mug    <- BI already knows

# =====================================================================
# STEP-BY-STEP, IN PLAIN ENGLISH
# =====================================================================
# 1. SharedDatabase is the heart of an ERP: ONE place that holds products,
#    sales and cash. It is the "single source of truth".
# 2. Each module (Sales, Inventory, Finance, BI) is handed the SAME
#    database object. They are different windows onto the same data, not
#    separate spreadsheets.
# 3. When Sales calls record_sale() ONCE, the database lowers the stock,
#    raises the cash, and stores the sale — all in that single place.
# 4. Afterwards, Inventory shows fewer mugs, Finance shows more cash, and
#    BI names the best seller — yet nobody re-entered the data. That is
#    exactly the benefit ERP gives a real company.
# 5. Compare this to the old way: if Sales, Inventory and Finance each kept
#    their own copy, they would quickly disagree. The shared database is
#    what keeps everyone honest and consistent.
# =====================================================================

Customer Relationship Management (CRM): the 360-degree view

Part D · Customer Relationship Management (CRM): the 360-degree view

If ERP is the system that looks inward at how a company runs, CRM (Customer Relationship Management) is the system that looks outward at the people it sells to. A CRM is software for tracking every customer and every interaction with them — calls, emails, purchases, support tickets, complaints — all gathered into one place so the business can build better, longer relationships.

The phrase to remember is the "360-degree view": one complete, shared record per customer, visible to everyone who needs it. Let's unpack the key components and properties.

The defining property: one shared customer record. Just like ERP centralises company data, CRM centralises customer data. When the sales rep, the marketing team, and the support agent all look up the same customer, they see the same full history. No more "let me transfer you" followed by re-explaining your problem from scratch.

The customer lifecycle. CRM systems are built to move people through stages, usually something like: Lead (a possible customer who's shown a flicker of interest) → Prospect (a qualified lead worth pursuing) → Customer (they bought something) → Loyal advocate (a repeat customer who recommends you). The CRM tracks where each person sits and helps nudge them to the next stage.

The three flavours of CRM. Textbooks usually split CRM into three cooperating styles:

  • Operational CRM — the day-to-day machinery: managing contacts, logging sales activity, automating marketing emails and support workflows.
  • Analytical CRM — the brains: studying the collected data to spot patterns, such as which customers are most valuable or most likely to leave (sometimes called churn).
  • Collaborative CRM — the connective tissue: sharing that customer information across departments so sales, marketing, and support all act on the same knowledge.

Why companies care: a CRM turns scattered sticky notes and personal inboxes into a shared memory of the customer. That means fewer dropped leads, more repeat business, and marketing that actually fits the person.

Who makes CRM software? The market leader is Salesforce, with HubSpot and Zoho also widely used, especially by smaller firms. Salesforce keeps a beginner-friendly explainer of what CRM is if you want a second take in different words.

One more system you'll hear named alongside ERP and CRM is SCM (Supply Chain Management) — software that manages the flow of goods from supplier to warehouse to customer. We won't go deep on it today, but the comparison table later puts all three side by side.

Part E

Architecture: how these systems are actually built

Part E · Architecture: how these systems are actually built

We've met the systems; now let's peek under the hood. "Architecture" just means how the software is organised into parts. You don't need to be a programmer to get this — the ideas are about tidiness and teamwork, not code.

The classic shape: three-tier architecture. Almost every enterprise system — ERP, CRM, and the rest — is split into three layers, called tiers:

  1. Presentation tier — the part you see and touch: the screens, buttons, and forms. Also called the UI (User Interface).
  2. Application tier — the "brain" that does the thinking: it holds the business rules, like "if stock falls below 100, suggest a re-order." Sometimes called the logic or middle tier.
  3. Data tier — the DB (Database) where all the facts are stored: customers, orders, inventory.

When you click a button, your request travels down from the presentation tier to the application tier (which figures out what to do) to the data tier (which fetches or stores the facts), and the answer travels back up to your screen.

Why bother splitting it up? Because separation keeps big systems sane. You can redesign the screens without touching the database. You can beef up security around the data tier on its own. Different specialists can work on different tiers at the same time. It's the software version of a well-organised kitchen: the dining room, the cooking line, and the pantry each have a clear job.

How the pieces are packaged. You'll hear three styles:

  • Monolithic — the whole application is built and shipped as one big block. Simple to start, but one small change means rebuilding the entire thing.
  • Modular — the application is divided into separate, swappable modules (remember ERP's modules). Tidier and easier to extend.
  • Microservices — the application is broken into many small, independent services that each do one job and talk to each other. Very flexible and scalable; this is how a lot of modern cloud software is built.

Where the system lives. Three options, and you'll meet all three:

  • On-premises — the company runs the software on its own servers, in its own building. Maximum control, but you maintain everything yourself.
  • Cloud — the software runs on a vendor's servers and you use it over the internet, often as SaaS (Software as a Service) that you rent monthly. Less control, far less hassle.
  • Hybrid — a mix: some parts kept in-house (often sensitive data), some in the cloud.

The glue: APIs and middleware. Real companies rarely buy everything from one vendor, so systems need to talk to each other. An API (Application Programming Interface) is an agreed set of rules that lets one piece of software request something from another — like a waiter taking your order to the kitchen using a fixed menu. Middleware is connector software that sits in the middle and helps different systems pass information back and forth. Together they let a CRM, an ERP, and an online store behave like one joined-up whole.

Real-life example — "What happens when you press the button"

What this example shows: a single click travelling down the three tiers and back up — the everyday journey of a request inside any enterprise system. How it ties to the lecture: it makes the three-tier idea concrete, so "presentation, application, data" stops being jargon and becomes a path you can trace with your finger.

You open your bank's website and click "Check balance." Here's the round trip:

  • Presentation tier: the web page registers your click and sends a request.
  • Application tier: the bank's logic checks you're logged in and allowed to see this account, then decides it needs your balance.
  • Data tier: the database looks up your account and returns the number.
  • Back up it goes — application tier formats it nicely, presentation tier shows "\$2,450.00" on your screen.

All of that in a fraction of a second. The reason the bank can keep your data locked down tight while still giving you a friendly screen is precisely because those jobs live in separate tiers. Separation isn't bureaucracy here — it's what makes the system safe, fast, and maintainable all at once.

function log(msg){
  // Add the new message on its own line at the bottom.
  consoleBox.textContent += "\n" + msg;

// Briefly highlight a tier so you can SEE the request arrive there.
function light(id){
  // Find the tier element by its id.
  const el = document.getElementById(id);
  // Switch the highlight on.
  el.classList.add("lit");
  // Switch it back off shortly after, for a pulse effect.
  setTimeout(() => el.classList.remove("lit"), 650);

// ---- TIER 2 logic: the application decides and queries the data ----
// This function is the "business logic". It never touches the screen and
// never stores data itself — it only decides and asks the data tier.
function applicationLogic(request){
  // The app receives the request from the presentation tier.
  log("APP : received request '" + request + "', querying data tier…");
  // Light up the data tier to show we are reading from it.
  light("t-data");
  // Read the answer from the data tier (the database object).
  if(request === "stock") return "We have " + DATABASE.mugStock + " mugs in stock.";
  if(request === "price") return "A mug costs $" + DATABASE.mugPrice.toFixed(2) + ".";
  // If the request is unknown, return a safe message.
  return "Sorry, I don't understand that request.";
}

// ---- TIER 1 action: the user clicks; the request starts its journey ----
function ask(request){
  // Stop new clicks while one trip is animating.
  setButtons(false);
  // Clear the previous result and reset the console.
  resultBox.textContent = "";
  consoleBox.textContent = "User clicked in the PRESENTATION tier.";
  // Highlight the presentation tier — the journey begins here.
  light("t-pres");

  // After a short pause, the request "arrives" at the application tier.
  setTimeout(() => {
    // Show the request moving down to the app tier.
    light("t-app");
    log("UI  : sending request down to application tier…");

    // After another pause, the app tier does its work and replies.
    setTimeout(() => {
      // Run the business logic, which reads the data tier.
      const answer = applicationLogic(request);

      // After a final pause, the answer travels back up to the screen.
      setTimeout(() => {
        // Highlight the presentation tier again — the answer is back.
        light("t-pres");
        log("APP : sending answer back up to the screen.");

        resultBox.textContent = "Answer: " + answer;
        // Re-enable the buttons for the next try.
        setButtons(true);
      }, 700);
    }, 700);
  }, 700);
}

How this page works, step by step (in plain English):

  1. The page draws three labelled boxes stacked vertically: Presentation on top, Application in the middle, Data at the bottom — the three tiers.
  2. When you click the button in the Presentation box, a small JavaScript function starts a request and writes "request sent" to the live log.
  3. The request visibly moves down: the Application tier lights up and the log says it's checking the business rules.
  4. The request reaches the Data tier, which "looks up" the answer and lights up in turn.
  5. The answer then travels back up the tiers to the Presentation box, where it appears on screen — and the log shows the full round trip you just watched.
  6. Because the whole thing is animated and narrated in one offline file, you can replay the journey as many times as you like until the three-tier idea feels obvious.
